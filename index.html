    Field lab

    Three tools for living on the surface: how sound behaves in Mars air, how a suited body moves at 38% gravity, and what it takes to print a shielded habitat from local soil.

    How the walk sounds on Mars

    Mars air is 95% carbon dioxide at under 1% of Earth's pressure. Perseverance's SuperCam microphone measured the result: sound travels at about 240 m/s, high pitches arrive slightly faster than low ones, and they fade within a few metres. Pick a sound and a distance and hear the difference.

    Speeds: about 240 m/s below 240 Hz and 250 m/s above, measured with SuperCam's microphone (Maurice et al., Nature, 2022). The thin air makes sound about 20 dB weaker. The high-frequency fade here is a simplified curve tuned to the team's finding that two people 5 m apart would struggle to talk. Sounds are synthesised in your browser. Hear NASA's real recordings.

    How a suited body moves at 38% gravity

    Walking is a pendulum, so lower gravity means slower, longer strides and an earlier switch to bounding. These numbers follow the Froude-number scaling used in reduced-gravity locomotion research, and they follow the slope of the Marswalk when the clock runs.

    Stride length uses Alexander's relation λ = 2.3·h·Fr0.3, with Fr = v²/(g·h). Walk-to-run switch at Fr ≈ 0.5, which held in reduced-gravity tests (Kram, Domingo and Ferris, J. Exp. Biol., 1997). Jump height assumes the same leg work as a 40 cm jump on Earth, now lifting body plus suit. Effort comes from the Marswalk's metabolic model.

    Print a regolith habitat

    Drag the dome on the 25 cm image and size it. QUIPU works out how much Martian soil a robot has to dig and sinter into the shell, and the solar power the printer needs. Every engineering number is editable.

    Dome = hemisphere. Shell mass = ⅔π((r+t)³ − r³) × density. Sintering energy assumes heating basalt-like soil to about 1,100 °C at roughly 25% efficiency. Mars's own atmosphere already gives about 20 g/cm² of shielding overhead; galactic cosmic rays cannot be stopped completely, so thicker walls give diminishing returns. Reference: NASA 3D-Printed Habitat Challenge.

    Moon: south pole

    The same planning ideas at the Artemis destination. Gravity is 16% of Earth's, the Sun never climbs more than about 1.5° above the horizon at the pole, and some crater floors have not seen sunlight in billions of years.

    Artemis III candidate regions

    NASA's October 2024 list: Peak near Cabeus B, Haworth, Malapert Massif, Mons Mouton Plateau, Mons Mouton, Nobile Rim 1, Nobile Rim 2, de Gerlache Rim 2 and Slater Plain. All lie within about 6° of the pole. They are listed, not pinned, because we could not verify their exact outlines.

    Deep Space Network, live

    NASA's three antenna complexes sit about 120° apart around Earth, so one always faces Mars. This refreshes every 10 seconds from DSN Now. Mars spacecraft are highlighted.

    Range and round-trip light time come from the antennas' own tracking. A data rate of 0 means a carrier or command link with no telemetry at that moment. Source: DSN Now, NASA JPL.
